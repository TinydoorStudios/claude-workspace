# Unit 04 — Snare bottom × Electro-Voice N/D 408 · CH 4

INSTRUMENT   Snare, BOTTOM head, claw-mounted on the rim (band's note: "claw"). Same drum as
             ch 3. What a bottom mic actually hears is the snare WIRES rattling against the
             resonant head — not the drum. Its whole contribution to this mix is sizzle and bite
             layered under ch 3's crack, which is exactly what a funk backbeat and a Motown
             two-and-four want. **Two-mic source with CH 3.**

MIC          **Electro-Voice N/D 408** — confirmed by Brian 2026-08-08: a bare "408" is always the
             EV, and he calls the Lauten LS-408 "the Lauten" or "snare mic". Vintage first-gen
             (no letter suffix), supercardioid N/DYM neodymium dynamic, 30 Hz–22 kHz.
             **DYNAMIC — 48V stays OFF.** (The band's blank 48V cell corroborated the identity.)
             No switches. Claw/rim mount, so no stand.
             ⚠ **POLARITY INVERT on this channel.** Not optional.

CAPSULE FACT **30 Hz–22 kHz supercardioid N/DYM dynamic** using a neodymium magnet EV rates at
             four times the power potential of conventional magnets (Electro-Voice N/D 408
             literature via Reverb listing), and it is a mic the community reaches for on "guitar
             cabs, snares and toms." The KB row adds the quantitative character that matters here:
             **brighter and more aggressive upper mids than an SM57**, a deliberate proximity
             lift, 30 Hz–22 kHz close / 60 Hz–22 kHz far.

WEB SAYS     On the polarity question the physics is unambiguous and externally documented: a
             stick hit moves the top head *away* from a top mic (negative pressure) while driving
             the bottom head *toward* the bottom mic (positive) — so the bottom mic is inverted
             relative to the top, every time (SOS Sound Advice; Sweetwater InSync). Sweetwater is
             also explicit that the bottom mic's job is the wires: "the sound of the actual
             snares… can be used very effectively to add brilliance and bite to the snare sound
             picked up from above." That is the lane, stated by an external source.

KB SAYS      mic-library: "Supercardioid N/DYM neodymium dynamic, 30 Hz–22 kHz close /
             60 Hz–22 kHz far, brighter and more aggressive upper mids than an SM57, deliberate
             proximity lift, high SPL, tiny pivoting head that fits where a 421 won't.
             Toms/cabs/snare. Weakness: that upper-mid bite gets spiky on bright sources; close
             working distance piles low-mids." Bias: ease off presence, attack; tame box ~400 Hz.

VERDICT      **AGREE.** The web establishes the mic's identity and the bottom-snare technique;
             the KB supplies the capsule character (aggressive upper mids, proximity low-mid
             pile) and neither contradicts the other. The KB is doing what it's for here —
             cross-checking a web pass, not substituting for it.

LOCKER       Silent pass. The fork was contingent on the identity question, and with the EV
             confirmed there is no free alternative that wins on a concrete, nameable margin: the
             DM17 (DK-6) is a rim-mount condenser and a real candidate, but it needs 48V on a
             channel the band deliberately left unpowered, and the EV's aggressive upper mids are
             an ASSET on a source whose entire job is wire bite. A tie goes to the specified mic.

GENRE BEND   Funk and Motown live on snare articulation — ghost notes, press rolls, rimshots. The
             bottom mic is where those read. So the wire lane gets a real lift (see gate check),
             which is the one place on this kit a boost is genuinely earned. Artist layer: they
             back gospel/R&B national acts, where the snare is crisp and forward rather than
             fat and vintage — supports the sizzle lane over a body lane.

VENUE BEND   FSQ outdoor: the box/pile cut runs FSQ-deep, and sits at **500 Hz — offset from
             ch 3's 400** so two mics on one drum don't dig an identical hole. The claw mount
             forces a very close working distance, and the KB names exactly that as the cause of
             low-mid pile, so the HPF runs HIGH at 200. Weather layer: 38 % RH and dry the whole
             set means the 9 kHz wire lift is not just cosmetic — it's the band that survives the
             throw to the back of the plaza, so it stays.

GATE CHECK   **One boost on this channel: B4 +3 @ 9000. The fact that permits it —**
             the ND408's documented voicing problem is in the UPPER MIDS (KB: "brighter and more
             aggressive upper mids than an SM57… that upper-mid bite gets spiky on bright
             sources"), i.e. roughly 2–5 kHz, and that is precisely where B3 is TRIMMING it at
             −6 @ 4000. There is no documented capsule peak at 9 kHz in either the EV literature
             (flat spec to 22 kHz) or the KB row, so the lift is landing on unvoiced response, not
             stacking on it. It is also the one zone ch 3 does not touch, so it does not stack
             across the two-mic pair either.
             - B3 −6 @ 4000 does double duty: tames the capsule's spiky upper mids AND keeps ch 4
               out of ch 3's baked 5.5 kHz crack lane.
             - B1 FLAT and HPF 200: no body on this channel at all. Ch 3 owns body via its baked
               +5 @ 150, and a claw-mounted mic this close would only contribute proximity mud.
             - ⚠ POLARITY INVERT, per the SOS/Sweetwater physics above. Without it this channel
               subtracts from ch 3 instead of adding to it, and no EQ fixes that.
