## Recommended Grit Sequence (verbatim)

- 220 grit – Defect removal / first cut
- 320 grit – Remove the first grit's scratches
- 400 grit – Refine for smoother finish
- 600 grit – Final refinement before sealing/polishing

## Why sanding matters (verbatim)

"On hard surfaces like tile, stone, and glass, skipping grits often leaves a scratch pattern that becomes more visible after cleaning, sealing, or polishing. A steady grit progression removes the previous grit's scratches and makes the finish look more uniform."

## Tools (verbatim)

- Sanding block (for edges) or flat backing (for small flats)
- Spray bottle with water (wet sanding reduces dust and helps control scratches)
- Microfiber cloths
- Masking tape (protect adjacent surfaces)
- Raking light to check haze and leftover scratches

## Pro tips (verbatim)

- "Use raking light often. It reveals haze and leftover scratches immediately."
- "Don't skip steps. Skipping grits often leaves scratches that reappear after cleaning or sealing."
- "Keep it wet (when appropriate). Wet sanding reduces dust and helps prevent loading."
- "Change sheets early. Fresh abrasive cuts cleaner and reduces random scratches."

## FAQ excerpt (verbatim)

"Wet or dry sanding? Wet sanding is often preferred to control dust and keep scratches consistent on hard surfaces."

"How do I avoid rounding edges? Use a backing block and keep pressure even."

## Note on laser-specific context (not from this source)

Separate laser-engraving-slate guides already in this KB (see [[2026-07-06_diode-laser-slate-white-yellow-forum-digest]]) note that sanding slate before engraving is often treated as optional by laser hobbyists — several sources say selecting naturally flat, unflecked stock matters more than sanding, and that a heavily polished/glassy surface can change how the laser's contrast mark reads compared to a clean matte finish. This is a laser-specific consideration layered on top of the general sanding technique above, not a claim from this RAW source.
