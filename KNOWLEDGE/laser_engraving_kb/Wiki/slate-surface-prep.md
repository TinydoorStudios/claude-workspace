# Slate Surface Prep — Sanding Without Scuff Marks

**Status:** established
**Last updated:** 2026-07-12
**Sources:** [[2026-07-12_slate-sanding-grit-sequence-equalle]], [[2026-07-06_diode-laser-slate-white-yellow-forum-digest]]

## Summary

Scuff marks left after sanding slate are almost always leftover scratch patterns from a skipped or rushed grit step, not a sign of the wrong grit choice. A steady wet progression from 220 through 600 grit, with even pressure and a check under raking light between stages, removes them cleanly. For slate headed to the laser specifically, there's no need to chase a polished finish beyond that.

## Body

**Grit sequence:** 220 (defect removal / first cut) → 320 (removes the 220's scratch pattern) → 400 (refine) → 600 (final refinement). Each grit's only job is to erase the previous grit's scratches — skipping a step leaves that coarser scratch pattern ghosting through once the surface is cleaned, oiled, or engraved, which is what usually reads as a "scuff mark."

**Wet, not dry.** A light mist of water at every stage controls dust (slate's silica dust is a real inhalation hazard dry) and keeps the scratch pattern more consistent than dry sanding does.

**Even pressure matters more than the grit number.** Use a sanding block or flat backing rather than bare-hand sanding — uneven hand pressure is what produces visible patchy scuffing, independent of which grit is loaded. A backing block also prevents unintentionally rounding edges.

**Check under raking (low-angle) light between grits**, not just at the end. It reveals leftover scratches immediately, while they're still cheap to fix by going back one step — waiting until final inspection means redoing more work.

**Swap sandpaper sheets often.** Slate dulls abrasive fast; a dulled sheet drags and burnishes instead of cutting cleanly, which is its own source of scuffing separate from grit choice.

**Stop at 400–600 for laser work, don't chase a polish.** This KB's laser-specific sources ([[slate-white-vs-yellow]]) note that many laser hobbyists treat sanding as optional entirely — flat, unflecked stock selection matters more to a clean engrave than surface polish — and that pushing to a glassy, heavily polished finish can change how the laser's white contrast mark reads compared to a clean matte surface. 600 grit gets a smooth, scuff-free surface without over-polishing.
