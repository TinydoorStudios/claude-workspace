1The Hero Number

Live screen capture, spl.tinydoorstudios.com — Simulator mode, Fountain Square

The big number top-left is the current reading — a 10-second rolling average (labeled 10-s LAeq) from our one calibrated measurement microphone at front-of-house. Think of it as a speedometer: it's telling you what's happening right now, smoothed just enough that it isn't jumping around on every kick drum hit.

Next to it is the limit — our internal working ceiling for the show (90 dBA in this example). The row of segments is a headroom gauge, centered on that limit line: it fills green to the right as long as we're under the limit, and more lit segments means more buffer before we'd cross it. If we went over, segments on the "OVER" side (left) would light up instead, showing how far past the limit we'd gone. Headroom, top right, is that same buffer expressed as one number — the gap left before we'd hit the limit.

Why a 10-second average. A single instant reading bounces all over the place and isn't useful to make decisions from. Ten seconds is short enough to react to in real time, long enough to actually mean something.

2Virtual Readings — and Why They're Trustworthy

SPL Compensation Measurement row — Front of Stage is the one physical mic; everything else is calculated

We only have one physical, calibrated measurement microphone out there, positioned at front-of-house. Every other card on that row — Middle of Plaza, Back of Plaza, Container Bar, The Davidson, Jeff Ruby's, McCormick & Schmick's, Westin Hotel — is a virtual reading. There's no second microphone sitting on the patio at Jeff Ruby's. Those numbers are calculated.

Here's how that calculation stays honest: our measurement software (Smaart) supports a feature called SPL Compensation. A reference signal gets measured at both the FOH mic position and each of those other spots, and the software works out a fixed dB offset between them. From then on, that offset rides along with the live FOH reading, so each card shows what a microphone placed at that spot actually would read — without needing a microphone actually placed there.

That's also why every card is labeled "SPL Compensation · Reference Only" right on the dashboard itself. That's not a caveat we added for this guide — it's built into the software: calibrated estimates anchored to one real physical measurement, not a second mic quietly sitting at each spot.

Note: this offset is set during a calibration pass against a physical reference standard — it isn't something we redo before every single show.

3Two Numbers That Matter: 10-Second vs. 6-Minute

The compliance strip — our working limit and the city's separate ordinance number, side by side

The dashboard tracks two different limits, and they're easy to mix up:

Our working limit
90 dBA, measured as a 10-second average at FOH. This is the number we watch to make mix decisions and the number that drives the strike system (more on that next page). It's ours — a mixing guardrail, not a legal one.

City ordinance
75 dBA, measured as a 6-minute rolling average at the property line. This is Cincinnati's actual Downtown Development Zone sound ordinance number — the one that matters legally, and it's measured out at the edge of the site, not at our mix position.

A loud moment at FOH can spike the 10-second number without ever pushing the 6-minute property-line average anywhere near the ordinance limit — that's normal. The dashboard estimates that property-line number continuously so we're never guessing:

Sound Ordinance panel — FOH is a live measurement; the surrounding property lines are calculated estimates (see previous page)

FOH is tagged "Live FOH" because it's the real, direct measurement. The property-line figures (Westin Hotel, The Davidson, Jeff Ruby's) are tagged "Estimated" because they're calculated the same way the virtual readings on the previous page are — offset from the one physical mic, not a second mic on someone else's roof.

4The Three-Strike System

Strike tracking panel — live counter, current status, total time over limit, and the most recent violation

Every time the 10-second FOH reading crosses our working limit (90 dBA in this example), it's logged as a strike, tracked toward a maximum of three. The panel above shows exactly what that looks like mid-show: 3 strikes logged, status currently clear (meaning we're back under the limit right now), and a running total of 2:32 of total time spent over the limit across the whole night.

Duration is tracked per incident, not just counted. Last violation: #3 · 45s · 94 dBA tells us strike three lasted 45 seconds and peaked at 94 dBA — so we know not just that it happened, but how long it ran and how hot it got. That's what lets us have a real conversation afterward instead of a vague one.

The Slack alert. Any strike of 3 or higher automatically posts to our internal Slack channel in real time, with the violation number, duration, and peak level included — the same detail shown on screen. Nobody has to be staring at this dashboard for a violation to get flagged and logged.

The Reset button clears the counter between shows — it doesn't erase the historical log, it just starts the next event's count at zero.

5Where the Data Comes From, and How to Check It Yourself

Full dashboard view — every panel covered in this guide, together in context
1Smaart (our field-standard SPL logging software) reads the FOH measurement mic continuously through the show. When it's a real show, the dashboard shows the actual input source (a DiGiCo console feed); in these sample screens it reads "Simulator" instead, so you can tell at a glance that this data isn't from a live event.
2Smaart streams that live data out continuously, which is what powers the dashboard at spl.tinydoorstudios.com in real time — the page you're looking at updates the whole time doors are open.
3Every night, Smaart separately generates and emails a full SPL log report — an independent record of the night, separate from the live dashboard feed, so we've still got a log if the connection ever drops mid-show.

The live dashboard and the report that lands in the inbox the next morning trace back to the same measurement chain — just two different snapshots of it.

Access. The dashboard is browser-based at spl.tinydoorstudios.com — it opens on any laptop, tablet, or phone with an internet connection, no software install needed. The venue selector at top lets us switch between monitored sites as more come online.
